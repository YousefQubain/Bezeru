Has Luxury Watchmaking Become Too Expensive?

Luxury watches were never supposed to be inexpensive. But as prices continue to rise and the industry concentrates more attention on its wealthiest customers, a reasonable question emerges: are we paying for better watchmaking, or simply for the privilege of participating in it?

There is something strange about discussing the price of a luxury watch.

On one hand, nobody expects a finely made mechanical timepiece to cost the same as an ordinary consumer product.

The engineering, finishing, materials and specialized knowledge involved can be extraordinary.

On the other hand, I sometimes find myself looking at a relatively straightforward steel watch and wondering how we arrived at its asking price.

Not because the watch is necessarily unattractive.

Not because the movement is poorly made.

But because the distance between what the object offers and what the customer is expected to pay seems to have grown considerably.

And I don't think I'm alone in questioning it.

Luxury watchmaking has always involved prestige, exclusivity and a certain willingness to pay beyond practical necessity.

But something has changed in the way prices are discussed.

A five-figure price tag can now feel almost ordinary in certain corners of the industry.

A watch costing several thousand francs may be described as an accessible entry point.

And a particularly expensive release can sometimes be discussed more in terms of its allocation and scarcity than its design or mechanical achievements.

I understand that luxury has never been democratic.

But I wonder whether the industry has become so comfortable moving further upmarket that it risks forgetting what originally drew many enthusiasts toward mechanical watches.

The fascination of a movement.

The satisfaction of good design.

The pleasure of discovering something interesting without requiring a considerable financial commitment.

So perhaps the question isn't simply whether luxury watches have become too expensive.

It is whether the industry has become less interested in explaining why they deserve to cost what they do.

The Numbers Tell an Interesting Story

The Swiss watch industry provides a useful starting point.

According to the Federation of the Swiss Watch Industry, Swiss watch exports reached CHF 25.6 billion in 2025, a decline of 1.7% compared with the previous year.

The number of wristwatches exported fell more sharply, declining 4.8% to approximately 14.6 million units.

These figures are worth considering together.

The industry shipped fewer watches, while the total export value declined by a considerably smaller percentage.

That does not automatically mean every watch became more expensive.

Changes in product mix, currency movements and the performance of individual brands all influence the results.

And export prices should not be confused with the prices consumers pay in boutiques.

Still, the wider direction is difficult to ignore.

Swiss watchmaking has become increasingly concentrated around higher-value products, with a relatively small number of powerful brands commanding a significant share of the industry's commercial attention.

The contrast became especially visible in the 2025 figures.

Watches with export prices above CHF 3,000 remained central to the industry's overall value, while several lower-priced segments faced greater pressure.

The first half of 2026 offered a more complicated picture.

According to the Federation, export volumes increased slightly, partly supported by growth in lower-priced mechanical watches. Yet demand in the middle export-price segment remained under pressure.

By September, Swiss watch exports were showing renewed momentum, rising 11.7% compared with the same month in 2025.

For the first nine months of 2026, cumulative exports reached approximately CHF 19.5 billion, an increase of 2.7%.

So this is not a simple story of an industry in permanent decline.

There are signs of resilience.

But there is also a question about what kind of industry is becoming more resilient.

One that sells more watches to more people?

Or one increasingly dependent on a smaller group of customers prepared to spend considerably more?

I think that distinction deserves attention.

Because commercial success and the long-term health of watch collecting are not necessarily the same thing.

Expensive Does Not Automatically Mean Overpriced

Before criticizing luxury watch prices, I think it is important to acknowledge something obvious.

Good watchmaking can be expensive for legitimate reasons.

Developing and manufacturing a mechanical movement requires specialized engineering, machinery, testing and technical expertise.

Case construction can involve complicated manufacturing processes.

Decorative finishing may demand hours of skilled manual work.

And the cost of maintaining an effective after-sales service network can extend long beyond the initial sale.

These are real expenses.

A hand-finished movement produced in very limited quantities cannot reasonably be expected to cost the same as a mass-produced movement assembled through a highly automated process.

Nor should we assume that a watchmaker working on a complicated mechanical calibre can achieve the same economies of scale as a much larger manufacturer.

There is also a difference between producing a watch and building an organization capable of supporting that watch for decades.

A company needs trained watchmakers, replacement components, distribution arrangements and the financial stability to stand behind its products.

Those things matter.

Rolex, for example, emphasizes the long-term servicing of its watches and maintains an international network of specialized workshops.

The brand states that it provides parts and labour support for at least 35 years after a watch is withdrawn from its catalogue.

That commitment is part of the ownership proposition.

But acknowledging legitimate costs does not mean every retail price is automatically justified.

A luxury watch's price also reflects branding, positioning, distribution, marketing and the level of profit a company believes its customers are willing to support.

That is not inherently dishonest.

Luxury businesses are not charities.

But I would prefer the conversation around expensive watches to distinguish between the cost of creating something remarkable and the commercial value of being associated with a particular name.

Sometimes those two things are closely connected.

Sometimes they are not.

And the difference is worth examining.

The Price of Prestige

Consider two watches.

Both use stainless-steel cases.

Both have automatic movements.

Both display the time and date.

One sells for several thousand francs.

The other costs several times as much.

It would be easy to assume the more expensive watch must be mechanically superior.

But that conclusion is not always supported by the specifications.

The difference might involve movement architecture, finishing, case construction, production volumes or quality control.

Those are meaningful distinctions.

But it might also involve brand recognition, perceived exclusivity and the commercial positioning of the product.

A familiar logo can carry considerable value.

Not simply because of advertising, but because a strong brand may have spent generations establishing trust, recognition and cultural relevance.

That history matters.

Still, I sometimes wonder whether prestige has become such an important part of luxury watch pricing that it risks overshadowing the actual object.

A watch becomes desirable because of the name on its dial.

The name becomes more desirable because its watches are expensive.

And the price itself begins to function as evidence of quality.

It is an effective commercial cycle.

But it can also make the watch difficult to judge independently.

Would the same design be celebrated as enthusiastically if it came from a lesser-known manufacturer?

Would the same movement receive as much attention?

Would collectors describe the same proportions as elegant, or would they suddenly find the watch too ordinary?

I think those questions reveal how much of our understanding of luxury is shaped by context.

There is nothing wrong with appreciating prestige.

But prestige should not become a substitute for curiosity.

When the Middle Becomes Difficult

One of the areas I find most interesting is the space between relatively accessible mechanical watches and the industry's most prestigious names.

There are still manufacturers producing capable, thoughtfully designed watches at prices well below the highest luxury segment.

Longines offers an interesting example.

Its Spirit Zulu Time combines a useful dual-time function with a COSC-certified movement and a silicon balance spring.

It is a serious mechanical watch with a clear purpose, rather than simply an inexpensive imitation of something more prestigious.

Grand Seiko offers another perspective.

Its 9F quartz movements demonstrate that precision, careful engineering and thoughtful finishing do not have to depend exclusively on mechanical complexity.

Certain 9F movements are rated to an accuracy of approximately ten seconds per year.

That is a remarkable level of performance.

And it challenges the assumption that a movement deserves admiration only if it operates through traditional mechanical means.

There are also smaller manufacturers offering distinctive designs, transparent specifications and an approach to watchmaking that does not rely on being recognized immediately across a room.

None of these alternatives is perfect.

A more affordable watch can still have disappointing finishing, uncomfortable proportions or compromises in its construction.

And a higher price can genuinely reflect superior execution.

But the existence of compelling alternatives makes me question the idea that price and quality always move together.

Perhaps the more important consideration is how much thought has gone into the watch.

Does the design make sense?

Is the movement appropriate?

Are the proportions convincing?

Does the bracelet feel well considered?

Will the watch be serviceable?

And does the product offer something worth appreciating beyond the status of its manufacturer?

These are questions that matter at almost every price level.

They also provide a more interesting basis for collecting than simply moving toward increasingly expensive brands.

The Costs Are Real, But So Is the Marketing

It would be unfair to ignore the economic pressures facing Swiss watchmakers.

The strength of the Swiss franc has made exports more expensive in several international markets.

Changes in trade policy have complicated pricing decisions.

And rising gold prices have increased the cost of producing watches using precious metals.

The Federation of the Swiss Watch Industry has repeatedly identified currency conditions, tariffs and material costs as challenges facing the sector.

These pressures should not be dismissed.

But I would also be cautious about treating every price increase as an unavoidable consequence of manufacturing costs.

Luxury pricing is a strategic decision.

A manufacturer may increase prices to protect margins.

It may adjust prices to reflect exchange rates.

Or it may use higher prices to reinforce a particular position within the market.

Often, several factors operate simultaneously.

The difficulty for customers is knowing how much of an increase reflects genuine economic pressure and how much reflects a brand's confidence in its pricing power.

And perhaps that is not a distinction companies are particularly interested in making visible.

Luxury marketing rarely focuses on manufacturing efficiency, commercial margins or distribution economics.

It emphasizes heritage, craftsmanship, rarity and emotion.

Those qualities can be entirely legitimate.

But they are also difficult to quantify.

A beautiful photograph of a hand-finished movement tells us something about the object.

It does not necessarily explain the full retail price.

Similarly, an advertisement celebrating a brand's history can provide cultural context without demonstrating why a particular reference costs more than a technically comparable watch.

I do not expect luxury watchmakers to disclose every element of their pricing strategy.

But I do think collectors should remain willing to ask questions.

Appreciating watchmaking does not require accepting every price without examination.

The Industry Risks Confusing Exclusivity With Desirability

There is another consequence of rising prices that I find particularly interesting.

As a watch becomes more expensive, its perceived exclusivity can increase.

That exclusivity may make the product more attractive to certain customers.

But it can also change the kind of attention the watch receives.

Instead of discussing the movement or design, people begin talking about access.

Who can obtain one?

How long is the waiting list?

Which clients receive priority?

And what does ownership communicate about someone's position within the world of luxury?

These conversations are not necessarily new.

Prestige has always played a role in high-end collecting.

But I think the emphasis has become more visible.

A watch can begin to function as proof that its owner belongs to a particular group.

And the object itself becomes secondary.

I find that unfortunate.

Some of the most interesting watches are not immediately recognizable.

Their appeal lies in details that require attention.

An unusual case construction.

An unconventional movement.

A historical connection.

A dial that becomes more compelling the longer you examine it.

These qualities do not necessarily require enormous prices.

Nor do they depend on the approval of a particularly exclusive retail environment.

I would argue that an industry concerned primarily with exclusivity risks making watch collecting less intellectually interesting.

Because the conversation gradually shifts away from what makes a watch good.

And toward what makes it difficult to obtain.

We Should Talk About Ownership, Not Just Purchase Price

There is also a practical side to luxury watch pricing that deserves more attention.

Buying the watch is only the beginning.

Mechanical watches require maintenance.

Components can wear.

Cases and bracelets may need attention.

And complicated movements can involve significant servicing costs.

Rolex, for example, publishes indicative servicing prices that vary depending on the model, generation and work required.

This is a useful reminder that the cost of ownership extends beyond the initial transaction.

The same principle applies across the industry.

A watch can appear relatively attractive at the point of purchase but become considerably more expensive to maintain over time.

Replacement components may become difficult to source.

Certain specialist movements may require servicing through limited networks.

And some vintage or complicated watches can require a substantial financial commitment simply to keep them functioning properly.

I think long-term serviceability should be considered part of the value proposition.

A watch designed to last decades should be supported accordingly.

And a brand asking customers to make a significant financial commitment should take that responsibility seriously.

Perhaps the real luxury is not simply owning an expensive watch.

It is owning something that remains meaningful, usable and supported long after its original purchase.

That is a form of value I find considerably more convincing than a high retail price alone.

Can a Watch Still Surprise Us Without Costing a Fortune?

One of the things I appreciate most about watchmaking is how much interest can exist within a relatively small object.

A watch does not need an elaborate complication to be compelling.

It might have unusually balanced proportions.

A distinctive dial texture.

A movement with a thoughtful technical solution.

Or a design that reflects a particular period in watchmaking history.

These details can create an attachment that is difficult to explain through price alone.

And I think this is where the industry still has an opportunity.

There is room for companies that make watchmaking exciting without treating increasingly high prices as the primary evidence of progress.

There is room for accessible mechanical watches that are honest about what they offer.

There is room for beautifully executed quartz watches.

And there is room for independent manufacturers that distinguish themselves through originality rather than prestige.

None of this requires the disappearance of ultra-high-end watchmaking.

I would not want that either.

Some of the industry's most extraordinary achievements depend on levels of specialized craftsmanship that will always be expensive.

But I do not think the success of watchmaking should be measured solely by how much its most prestigious manufacturers can charge.

A healthy industry also needs customers who are developing their interest.

People buying their first serious watch.

People discovering unfamiliar brands.

And collectors whose enthusiasm comes from design and engineering rather than financial status.

If those customers gradually feel that the most interesting part of watchmaking exists beyond their reach, the industry risks weakening the enthusiasm that supports its wider culture.

Has Luxury Watchmaking Become Too Expensive?

I don't think there is a single answer.

Some watches are expensive because producing them requires extraordinary skill, time and technical capability.

Others carry prices that reflect a combination of those qualities and the commercial value of a prestigious name.

And some, in my opinion, are difficult to justify when examined beyond their branding.

But perhaps the larger problem is not that expensive watches exist.

It is that the industry sometimes appears increasingly comfortable presenting high prices as though they were achievements in themselves.

A price can communicate scarcity.

It can communicate prestige.

It can establish a product within a particular luxury category.

But it cannot tell us whether the watch is beautifully designed.

It cannot guarantee that the movement is interesting.

And it cannot explain whether the object will remain enjoyable to own.

Those judgments require us to look beyond the number on the price tag.

I would rather see a watchmaker defend its pricing through the quality of its work than through the exclusivity of its customer base.

I would rather see an interesting movement than another carefully manufactured story about rarity.

And I would rather see collectors remain curious about watches at every price level than assume that the most expensive examples must also be the most worthwhile.

Luxury watchmaking has never needed to be inexpensive to be meaningful.

But it does need to give people reasons to care.

Good design.

Thoughtful engineering.

Lasting craftsmanship.

A sense that someone genuinely considered what the watch should be, rather than simply what the market would be willing to pay for it.

Those qualities have always been at the heart of the most compelling watchmaking.

And none of them can be measured by price alone.
