Who Decided Watches Needed a Gender?

As the watch industry begins moving away from men's and women's categories, a more interesting question emerges: were watches ever the problem, or was it how we chose to design and market them?

Walk into almost any luxury watch boutique and you will encounter a familiar distinction.

Men's watches on one side. Women's watches on the other.

The men's collection will typically feature larger cases, mechanical complications and sportier designs. The women's collection will often be smaller, more decorative and more likely to feature diamonds.

There are exceptions, of course.

But the distinction has shaped how watches have been designed, marketed and sold for generations.

Now, that approach is beginning to change.

For its 2026 edition, the Grand Prix d'Horlogerie de Genève (GPHG), one of watchmaking's most prestigious awards, has replaced its gender-based categories with classifications focused on the watches themselves.

The traditional men's and women's divisions have given way to categories including Essential Watch and Complication Watch, alongside a new Gemset category.

It is a relatively simple change.

But it raises a much bigger question about an industry that has spent decades telling us what kind of watch we are supposed to wear.

Personally, I think the change is overdue.

Not because men's and women's watches should suddenly disappear.

But because I have never understood why a watch's intended audience should be more important than its design.

When Did Size Become a Gender?

The idea that smaller watches are feminine and larger watches are masculine seems almost natural today.

But watchmaking history tells a more complicated story.

Consider the original Royal Oak.

When Audemars Piguet introduced Gérald Genta's design in 1972, its 39mm steel case was considered unusually large. Its visible screws, integrated bracelet and industrial appearance challenged traditional ideas of luxury.

Four years later, the brand introduced the Royal Oak II, a 29mm version designed by Jacqueline Dimier.

It would be easy to describe the watch as a smaller Royal Oak intended for women.

But that overlooks the work involved.

Dimier had to reconsider the proportions, case architecture and relationship between the bezel and bracelet to preserve the original design's identity at a much smaller size.

The watch also retained an automatic movement, Caliber 2062, at a time when fitting a self-winding mechanism into such a compact case presented significant technical challenges.

This was not simply a matter of reducing the dimensions.

It required a new approach to the same design.

And I think that distinction remains relevant today.

A smaller watch should not automatically be treated as a lesser version of a larger one.

In many cases, achieving the same balance, finishing and mechanical performance in a compact format requires just as much thought, if not more.

Yet the industry has often presented smallness as a matter of style rather than engineering.

Perhaps that is one of the assumptions worth reconsidering.

Cartier Understood Something Others Missed

If there is one brand that demonstrates why watches do not need to be restricted by gender, it is Cartier.

Look at the Panthère.

Introduced in the 1980s, the watch sits somewhere between traditional watchmaking and jewellery.

Its square case, Roman numerals, elegant bracelet and compact proportions create an identity that is immediately recognisable.

Nothing about the design depends on mechanical complexity.

Most Panthère models use quartz movements.

And that is completely fine.

The appeal comes from how the watch sits on the wrist, how the bracelet moves and how the case becomes part of the overall design.

Cartier has historically positioned the Panthère as a feminine creation, and that identity remains part of its appeal.

But the object itself does not stop being beautiful when someone outside its intended audience decides to wear it.

That is what interests me.

The watch has a strong enough design language to exist beyond the category assigned to it.

And Cartier is hardly alone.

The Tank has spent generations moving between different styles, personalities and wardrobes without requiring a fundamental redesign every time its audience changes.

Personally, I find this much more convincing than watching brands announce that a particular model is now unisex.

A watch does not become more versatile simply because the marketing department changes the description.

Its design has to make that versatility possible.

The Problem With Simply Adding Diamonds

There is another side to this conversation.

For years, certain watchmakers approached women's collections by taking an existing design, reducing its case size, changing the dial colour and adding diamonds.

The result was not necessarily unattractive.

Some of these watches are beautifully executed.

But it created a rather limited understanding of what women might want from high-end watchmaking.

The assumption seemed to be that men were interested in complications, while women were interested in decoration.

Of course, plenty of women appreciate jewellery watches. And jewellery itself involves extraordinary levels of craftsmanship.

The problem was never the presence of diamonds.

It was the suggestion that diamonds could substitute for mechanical ambition.

Patek Philippe offers an interesting example of how these expectations have evolved.

The Twenty~4 collection launched in 1999 with a distinctive rectangular design and quartz movement, created specifically for women.

Nearly two decades later, in 2018, Patek Philippe introduced the Twenty~4 Automatic.

The new model featured a round case, a self-winding mechanical movement and a very different design approach.

It remained a collection developed for women.

But it offered a broader interpretation of what that could mean.

To me, this is where the conversation becomes more useful.

The goal should not be to make every watch gender-neutral.

It should be to avoid assuming that a particular type of customer must prefer one kind of watchmaking over another.

Someone might appreciate diamonds and mechanical complications.

Someone else might prefer a simple quartz watch with excellent proportions.

Neither preference needs to be explained through gender.

Bulgari Makes a More Interesting Argument

One of the strongest recent examples comes from Bulgari.

The Serpenti has always occupied an unusual position in watchmaking.

Its bracelet wraps around the wrist, its case draws inspiration from a serpent's head, and its identity is closely connected to jewellery design.

It does not look like a conventional luxury watch.

That is precisely why it works.

In 2025, Bulgari introduced the Lady Solotempo BVS100, an in-house automatic movement developed for its Serpenti collection.

The movement measures just 19mm in diameter and offers approximately 50 hours of power reserve.

These figures matter because they demonstrate a technical commitment to a watch that is often appreciated primarily for its appearance.

Bulgari could have continued relying entirely on the strength of the Serpenti's design.

Instead, it invested in developing a mechanical movement suited to the collection's particular architecture.

I find that much more interesting than another supposedly gender-neutral watch launch.

Because Bulgari has not tried to make the Serpenti look like a traditional sports watch.

It has preserved the design's identity while expanding what exists inside it.

There is a lesson here.

Making watchmaking more inclusive does not have to mean making every watch look the same.

Sometimes it means taking an established design seriously enough to give it the technical development it deserves.

Removing Labels Is the Easy Part

Which brings us back to the GPHG.

I support the decision to move away from gender-based categories.

A watch should be judged on its design, construction, technical achievement and originality rather than assumptions about who is expected to wear it.

But I also think removing those categories is only the beginning.

There is a risk that the industry celebrates the idea of gender-neutral watchmaking without reconsidering how its products are actually developed.

If a brand removes the distinction between men's and women's collections but continues designing everything around one standard wrist size, what has really changed?

Similarly, if smaller models receive less mechanical attention, fewer complications or a narrower range of materials, the problem has not disappeared.

It has simply become less visible.

There is also a danger in assuming that creating watches specifically for women is somehow outdated.

I do not agree with that either.

A collection developed with a particular customer in mind can be genuinely innovative.

The Royal Oak II proved that in 1976.

Bulgari's modern Serpenti collection continues to demonstrate it.

The important distinction is whether the design responds to actual preferences and requirements, or simply reproduces familiar assumptions.

And perhaps the industry could benefit from paying more attention to that difference.

The Watch Should Come First

Ultimately, I think watch collecting becomes much more interesting when we stop treating certain designs as belonging exclusively to certain people.

A 34mm gold dress watch can be just as compelling on a man's wrist as a 40mm steel sports watch can be on a woman's.

But the reverse is equally true.

There is nothing wrong with wanting a traditionally masculine sports watch or a delicate jewellery piece.

The issue begins when those choices are presented as the only ones available.

Personally, I would rather see watchmakers focus on creating better-designed watches across a wider range of dimensions, materials and mechanical configurations.

Give collectors more choices.

Invest in smaller mechanical movements where they make sense.

Take jewellery watches seriously as objects of design and craftsmanship.

And allow people to decide what suits their wrists, wardrobes and personalities.

Because the most memorable watches have rarely been defined by the categories they were placed in.

The Cartier Panthère is not interesting because it was designed for women.

It is interesting because it is a beautiful piece of design.

The Royal Oak II matters because Jacqueline Dimier successfully reinterpreted an already radical watch without compromising its identity.

And Bulgari's Serpenti deserves attention because it approaches watchmaking from a completely different design perspective.

These are the qualities worth celebrating.

The watch industry has spent generations creating extraordinary objects, then occasionally limiting how we are supposed to appreciate them.

Perhaps the real opportunity is not to eliminate every distinction between men's and women's watches.

It is to stop allowing those distinctions to determine what a watch can be.

Good design has never needed permission to cross boundaries.

And a great watch should not need a gender to justify its existence.
