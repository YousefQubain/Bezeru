The Forgotten Swiss Watch Brands Worth Rediscovering

Watchmaking history is full of names that once mattered enormously, then gradually disappeared from view. As historic Swiss brands return to the market, a more interesting question emerges: what does it actually mean to bring a watchmaker back?

Swiss watchmaking has a remarkable ability to remember its most successful names.

Rolex, Patek Philippe, Omega, Audemars Piguet. Their histories are documented, celebrated and revisited with extraordinary consistency.

We know the watches that made them famous. We recognize their design languages. And even when their collections evolve, the connection to the past is rarely difficult to identify.

But Swiss watchmaking history is considerably larger than the brands that dominate today's conversations.

There were companies that developed important chronograph movements, built instruments for demanding environments, introduced unusual complications and contributed to the industry's technical progress without becoming permanent fixtures of modern luxury culture.

Some disappeared.

Others survived in reduced circumstances or through changes of ownership.

And a growing number have returned.

I find these stories particularly interesting because they challenge the way we tend to understand watchmaking heritage.

A brand's historical importance is not always reflected in its contemporary recognition.

Nor does a famous name necessarily guarantee that the company using it today has preserved everything that originally made it important.

The revival of a historic watchmaker can be a fascinating exercise in design, engineering and cultural preservation.

But it can also become an exercise in nostalgia.

And I think the distinction matters.

Because bringing back an old logo is relatively straightforward.

Bringing back the ideas that gave it meaning is something else entirely.

Universal Genève Was Never Just a Forgotten Name

Universal Genève is perhaps the most significant recent example.

Founded in 1894, the company developed a reputation for elegant chronographs, technically interesting movements and a distinctive approach to watch design.

Its catalogue included watches that continue to attract serious collectors today.

The Compax and Tri-Compax demonstrated the brand's involvement in chronograph development, while the Polerouter became one of the most recognizable designs of the 1950s.

Introduced in 1954, the Polerouter was designed by a young Gérald Genta, long before his name became inseparable from the Royal Oak and Nautilus.

Created in connection with Scandinavian Airlines' polar routes, the watch combined an elegant case with technical considerations suited to contemporary aviation.

Early examples used conventional automatic winding mechanisms, while later Polerouters adopted Universal Genève's celebrated micro-rotor movements.

The result was a watch that combined an unusually clear design identity with genuine mechanical interest.

And that is what makes the modern revival so compelling.

Universal Genève did not need someone to invent an identity for it.

The identity already existed.

What had disappeared was the brand's former position within contemporary watchmaking.

Following its acquisition in 2023, Universal Genève returned with new collections in April 2026, placing familiar names such as the Polerouter and Compax at the centre of its renewed offering.

The new Polerouter collection includes 37mm models alongside larger 39mm Polerouter Date versions, while the revived Compax revisits the brand's considerable chronograph heritage.

There is a great deal to admire in this approach.

The watches acknowledge their predecessors without simply reproducing them in every detail.

But the revival also raises a question I think is worth asking.

How much should a revived watchmaker change?

If the designs become too contemporary, they risk losing the character that made the originals interesting.

If they remain too faithful, the brand risks becoming a museum of its own history.

Universal Genève has a particularly difficult balance to strike because its past was not defined by one watch.

It was defined by a willingness to experiment with different forms, functions and movements.

The Polerouter matters.

The Compax matters.

But the brand's broader history of technical and aesthetic exploration matters just as much.

I would argue that the true measure of Universal Genève's revival will not be whether collectors approve of its first new Polerouter.

It will be whether the brand can eventually create something that becomes important in its own right.

Because the strongest tribute to a great watchmaker is not necessarily to repeat its most successful design.

It is to recover the confidence that made that design possible.

Nivada Grenchen and the Value of Knowing What You Are

Nivada Grenchen offers a rather different example.

Founded in 1926, the brand developed a reputation for practical watches designed around specific uses.

Its historic catalogue includes the Antarctic, Chronomaster Aviator Sea Diver and Depthmaster.

These were not watches built primarily around the modern language of luxury.

They were instruments.

The Chronomaster combined multiple timing functions within a distinctive sports chronograph design.

The Antarctic was connected to the era of polar exploration.

And the Depthmaster represented the industry's growing ambition to produce increasingly capable dive watches.

What interests me about Nivada Grenchen is that its identity was always relatively straightforward.

It made useful watches with recognizable designs.

The revival, led by Guillaume Laidet and Rémi Chabrat beginning in the late 2010s, embraced that history.

Rather than attempting to transform Nivada Grenchen into a completely different luxury proposition, the revived brand brought back familiar collections while updating their construction for contemporary use.

The Chronomaster retained its busy, instrument-like dial.

The Antarctic continued to emphasize the appeal of a restrained everyday watch.

And the Depthmaster preserved a design language that does not seem particularly interested in pleasing everyone.

I appreciate that.

There is a tendency within modern watchmaking to assume that every brand must become more prestigious, more expensive or more complicated in order to demonstrate progress.

But sometimes progress involves understanding what made a product desirable in the first place.

Nivada Grenchen's approach suggests that a historic design does not necessarily require dramatic reinterpretation to remain relevant.

It may simply need to be manufactured properly, offered in sensible proportions and presented to an audience that appreciates its original character.

The company celebrated its centenary in 2026, marking an interesting moment for a brand that spent many years outside the mainstream watchmaking conversation.

Of course, a revived company is not automatically identical to the business that existed a century earlier.

Ownership, manufacturing arrangements, suppliers and commercial circumstances can change significantly.

That distinction should not be overlooked.

But continuity can exist through design philosophy as well as corporate structure.

And Nivada Grenchen demonstrates how a clearly defined identity can survive a long interruption.

I find that more convincing than a revival that relies on heritage while trying to become something entirely unrelated to its origins.

Vulcain Had a Sound of Its Own

Then there is Vulcain.

Established in La Chaux-de-Fonds in the nineteenth century, the brand developed a particularly distinctive place in watchmaking history through its mechanical alarm watches.

The Vulcain Cricket, introduced in 1947, was designed to do something most wristwatches could not.

It could make noise.

Not merely the faint sound of a mechanical movement.

An actual alarm.

The Cricket's distinctive buzzing sound came from a mechanism designed to alert its wearer at a chosen time.

It was an unusual complication with an immediately understandable purpose.

And I think that is what makes the watch so appealing.

Mechanical complications are often discussed in terms of their difficulty, rarity or contribution to horological prestige.

But the Cricket reminds us that complexity can also serve a practical idea.

An alarm on the wrist was genuinely useful, particularly at a time when people did not carry smartphones or other electronic devices capable of performing the same task.

The Cricket also became associated with American presidents, contributing to Vulcain's enduring identity as the maker of the so-called Presidents' Watch.

Yet Vulcain's historical importance extends beyond that association.

The company's development of mechanical alarm movements represents a particular kind of watchmaking ambition.

It was concerned with adding a new function to an object people already wore every day.

Vulcain experienced periods of difficulty and revival, including a notable return in 2002.

Today, the Cricket remains central to the brand's identity.

But this is where I think the challenge becomes interesting.

A mechanical alarm is no longer a practical necessity for most people.

The technology has been surpassed in convenience by countless electronic alternatives.

So why should someone still want one?

Perhaps for exactly the reason that makes many traditional complications appealing.

The mechanism is interesting.

The sound is distinctive.

And the watch offers a physical experience that cannot be reduced to the function it performs.

I would not want Vulcain to abandon that identity simply because mechanical alarms have become less commercially relevant.

In fact, I think its unusual specialization is precisely what makes the brand worth rediscovering.

Not every watchmaker needs to compete by producing another luxury sports watch.

Some of the industry's most memorable creations came from companies that were willing to solve a different problem.

The Cricket is a reminder of that independence.

Excelsior Park: A Name Behind Other Names

Excelsior Park is perhaps the most revealing example of all.

Unlike a brand whose reputation rests primarily on a particular wristwatch design, Excelsior Park's significance is closely connected to the development and manufacturing of chronographs.

Its roots extend back to nineteenth-century watchmaking in Saint-Imier, Switzerland.

The company became known for stopwatches, chronograph mechanisms and precision timing instruments.

At various points in its history, Excelsior Park supplied chronograph movements to other watchmakers, including names such as Gallet, Zenith and Girard-Perregaux.

That is a particularly interesting detail.

Collectors often focus on the name printed on a dial.

But the history of Swiss watchmaking is full of companies whose technical contributions were not always visible to the people wearing the finished watches.

A famous brand might receive the recognition.

Another manufacturer might have provided an essential part of the mechanism.

And over time, the supplier's name could become considerably less familiar than those of its customers.

Excelsior Park reminds us that the industry's achievements were rarely the work of isolated companies.

They emerged from networks of designers, movement manufacturers, specialized workshops and technical suppliers.

The company eventually disappeared from the market following the difficult conditions of the late twentieth century.

Its modern revival began in 2021, with chronographs inspired by historical designs.

The revived watches used modern Swiss movements, including hand-wound Sellita chronograph calibres, rather than claiming to reproduce the original manufacturing operation exactly.

And that is an important distinction.

Reviving a chronograph specialist does not automatically restore the industrial capabilities that once defined it.

A company can reproduce an attractive case, dial and set of pushers without recreating the movement-development expertise of its predecessor.

That does not make the new watches meaningless.

But it does change what the revival represents.

Is it a continuation of technical innovation?

A preservation of design heritage?

Or a new business using an established historical identity?

I think these are fair questions.

Not because every revived brand must manufacture its own movements.

But because watchmaking enthusiasts should be able to appreciate historical significance without confusing it with contemporary technical capability.

The past deserves to be understood accurately.

And so does the present.

Heritage Is More Than an Old Logo

These four companies demonstrate why I find historic watch brand revivals so compelling.

They also reveal how differently a revival can be approached.

Universal Genève is attempting to reclaim a position associated with sophisticated design and technical ambition.

Nivada Grenchen has built its modern identity around recognizable, functional watches with strong connections to the original catalogue.

Vulcain continues to draw on a complication that made it unusually distinctive.

And Excelsior Park shows how the design heritage of a former movement specialist can be reintroduced even when the manufacturing circumstances have changed.

There is no single correct approach.

But I do think there is a difference between preserving heritage and simply using it.

A historic name can provide credibility.

It can attract collectors.

It can give a product an immediate narrative that a completely new watch company may have to spend years developing.

Yet that inherited recognition also creates a responsibility.

If a brand emphasizes its past, it should be honest about the relationship between its historical achievements and its contemporary products.

An old trademark is not the same thing as uninterrupted manufacturing.

A vintage-inspired chronograph is not necessarily built using the same movements or production methods as its predecessor.

And a reissued design does not become innovative merely because the original was.

These distinctions matter.

Not because collectors should reject revived brands.

Quite the opposite.

I think they make the most interesting revivals easier to appreciate.

When a company is clear about what it has preserved, what has changed and what it hopes to accomplish, the resulting story becomes considerably more convincing.

The past provides context.

It should not have to provide every answer.

The Problem With Rediscovering Everything

There is another side to the growing enthusiasm for forgotten watch brands.

Not every historical name deserves to return simply because it once existed.

Swiss watchmaking produced an extraordinary number of brands, references and manufacturing businesses over the course of the twentieth century.

Some made important contributions.

Others produced relatively ordinary watches.

And some are remembered today primarily because their names have become associated with an appealing vintage aesthetic.

That is perfectly understandable.

But nostalgia can make us less critical.

A beautifully aged dial, a compact case or an unfamiliar logo can create the impression that an object must possess some hidden historical significance.

Sometimes it does.

Sometimes it is simply an attractive old watch.

I think collectors should be comfortable with both possibilities.

Not every discovery needs to become a forgotten masterpiece.

And not every revival needs to be presented as the return of a legend.

There is value in appreciating modest watches for what they are.

There is also value in recognizing that the industry has always contained different levels of ambition, originality and technical achievement.

The same applies to companies returning today.

The historical catalogue might justify our curiosity.

But the contemporary watch still needs to justify our attention.

Its proportions should make sense.

Its movement should suit the product.

Its design should feel considered.

And its price should be understood in relation to what the buyer is actually receiving.

A famous old name cannot do all that work by itself.

What Makes a Revival Worth Remembering?

Ultimately, I think the best watch brand revivals do more than remind collectors of something they already liked.

They give the past a reason to remain relevant.

That does not necessarily mean abandoning familiar designs.

There is nothing wrong with bringing back a well-proportioned chronograph, a distinctive alarm watch or an elegant mid-century sports model.

Sometimes those designs have survived in collectors' memories precisely because they were good to begin with.

But successful preservation requires judgment.

What should remain unchanged?

What can be improved through modern manufacturing?

And which parts of the original identity deserve to influence the watches that come next?

These questions are much more interesting than simply asking whether a revived model looks faithful to a photograph from 1965.

I hope Universal Genève eventually develops new watches that become as distinctive as the Polerouter.

I hope Nivada Grenchen continues to demonstrate that functional design does not require exaggerated luxury positioning.

I hope Vulcain remains committed to the unusual mechanical character that made the Cricket important.

And I hope revivals such as Excelsior Park encourage collectors to look more carefully at the manufacturers and technical specialists whose contributions helped shape Swiss watchmaking.

Because rediscovering a brand should involve rediscovering its actual history.

Not just its logo.

Swiss watchmaking has never belonged exclusively to the companies that became household names.

Some of its most interesting ideas emerged from smaller manufacturers, specialist workshops and businesses whose reputations did not survive every change in the industry.

Their stories deserve attention.

But the responsibility of a revived watchmaker is not simply to convince us that the past was remarkable.

It is to demonstrate that something meaningful can still be created from it.

A historic name may be enough to make someone look twice.

What comes next is what determines whether they keep looking.
