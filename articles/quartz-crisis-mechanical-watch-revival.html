The Quartz Crisis Didn't Kill Watchmaking. It Changed Its Meaning.

The quartz revolution nearly dismantled the Swiss watch industry. But in making accurate timekeeping accessible to almost everyone, it also forced mechanical watchmaking to answer a more interesting question: what is a watch worth when accuracy is no longer enough?

There is a familiar way of telling the story of the quartz crisis.

Japanese manufacturers introduced a more accurate technology. Swiss watchmakers struggled to compete. Traditional mechanical watches came dangerously close to disappearing, before the industry rediscovered its heritage and found a way to survive.

It makes for a compelling story.

But I think it is also an incomplete one.

The quartz crisis was devastating for Swiss watchmaking. Companies disappeared, factories closed, and thousands of people lost their jobs. Generations of manufacturing knowledge were put at risk.

None of that should be overlooked.

Yet the idea that quartz almost killed watchmaking assumes that mechanical watches and quartz watches were fighting to achieve exactly the same thing.

For a long time, perhaps they were.

Both existed to tell the time, and the better watch was often understood to be the one that performed that task more accurately and reliably.

Quartz changed that relationship.

It made precise timekeeping increasingly accessible, affordable and convenient.

And once that happened, mechanical watches needed a reason to exist beyond their ability to keep time.

I would argue that this change, rather than the survival of mechanical movements alone, was the quartz revolution's most important legacy.

The Astron Wasn't the Enemy

On December 25, 1969, Seiko introduced the Quartz Astron 35SQ.

It was the world's first commercially available quartz wristwatch, and its precision represented an extraordinary technological achievement.

According to Seiko, the original Astron offered accuracy of approximately five seconds per month, a remarkable improvement over the performance expected from conventional mechanical watches of the period.

But there is something interesting about the original Astron that often gets lost in discussions of the quartz crisis.

It was not cheap.

The watch was housed in an 18-karat gold case and launched at a price of 450,000 yen, comparable to the cost of some cars in Japan at the time.

This was not initially a disposable electronic accessory.

It was an expensive expression of advanced watchmaking.

And that distinction matters.

We often discuss quartz as though it appeared fully formed as an inexpensive alternative to traditional craftsmanship. In reality, developing a reliable quartz wristwatch required years of engineering, miniaturization and technical experimentation.

Seiko had to overcome challenges involving the quartz oscillator, electronic circuits, power consumption and the motor responsible for moving the hands.

The result was an entirely different approach to measuring time.

I find it difficult to look at that achievement and see anything other than serious watchmaking.

It may not have involved the same skills required to manufacture a mechanical escapement, but it demanded considerable ingenuity of its own.

The Astron did not represent the absence of craftsmanship.

It represented a different kind of it.

The Crisis Was Bigger Than Quartz

One of the more misleading aspects of the quartz crisis narrative is the suggestion that Switzerland simply refused to innovate while Japan moved forward.

The reality was more complicated.

Swiss researchers were also developing quartz wristwatch technology during the 1960s. The Centre Electronique Horloger in Neuchâtel had produced working prototypes, and Swiss manufacturers introduced commercial quartz models in the early 1970s.

The technological possibilities were not entirely unknown.

The larger challenge involved how an industry built around mechanical manufacturing could adapt to a fundamentally different production model.

Swiss watchmaking depended on a complex network of manufacturers, component suppliers and specialized workshops.

Quartz changed the economics of that system.

As electronic movements became easier to manufacture at scale, accurate watches could be produced at prices that placed enormous pressure on traditional manufacturers.

At the same time, the Swiss industry faced other difficulties, including currency appreciation, rising costs and broader economic instability.

The consequences were severe.

According to the Fondation Haute Horlogerie, approximately six out of ten workers in the Swiss watch industry lost their jobs between 1970 and 1985.

That is an extraordinary human and industrial cost.

It is important to remember this when describing the period as a necessary transformation.

For the people whose livelihoods disappeared, the crisis was not an abstract debate about the future of luxury.

It was the collapse of an industry around which entire communities had been built.

And this is why I would be cautious about claiming that quartz somehow saved watchmaking.

Quartz did not save the industry from suffering.

It forced an already vulnerable industrial structure to confront changes it could no longer avoid.

What emerged afterward was not simply the old industry restored to its previous condition.

It was something different.

Swatch Understood What Had Changed

If there is one example that demonstrates the complexity of Switzerland's response, it is Swatch.

Introduced in 1983, the original Swatch was not an attempt to defend mechanical watchmaking against quartz.

It embraced quartz technology.

The watch was designed around efficient automated production, a simplified construction of just 51 components, and a price that made Swiss-made watches accessible to a much broader audience.

But what interests me most is that Swatch did not compete through technology alone.

It understood that a watch could also be an expression of personality.

Colour, graphics, design collaborations and playful collections became central to the product's identity.

A Swatch was not necessarily something someone purchased because they needed to know the time.

It could be bought because they liked its design, wanted another colour or simply enjoyed the idea of wearing something different.

That represented an important shift.

Quartz had made accuracy increasingly ordinary.

Swatch responded by making the watch itself interesting in another way.

And I think there is a parallel here with what happened at the higher end of mechanical watchmaking.

The products were very different, but the underlying question was similar.

If accurate timekeeping was no longer enough to distinguish a watch, what else could make someone want one?

Swatch answered through accessibility, creativity and personal expression.

Traditional mechanical watchmakers increasingly emphasized craftsmanship, heritage, technical complexity and exclusivity.

Neither response was inevitable.

Both required the industry to reconsider what it was actually selling.

Mechanical Watchmaking Found Another Reason to Exist

One of the most interesting developments of the 1980s was the renewed emphasis on mechanical complications.

At a time when electronic movements were becoming increasingly capable, certain manufacturers continued to invest in mechanical watchmaking.

Blancpain provides a particularly useful example.

In 1983, the brand introduced a mechanical complete-calendar watch with a moonphase display.

The watch did not challenge quartz on accuracy.

It offered something else.

Its appeal lay in the mechanism, the finishing, the traditional calendar indications and the pleasure of seeing mechanical complexity expressed through a relatively restrained design.

This was a very different way of arguing for the value of a watch.

A quartz movement could provide accurate timekeeping with comparatively little intervention from its owner.

A mechanical movement required an intricate arrangement of springs, gears, wheels and regulating components to perform the same basic task.

From a purely functional perspective, that complexity could appear unnecessary.

But perhaps necessity was no longer the point.

People do not always value objects because they offer the shortest or most efficient route to a result.

Sometimes the method matters as much as the outcome.

A hand-finished mechanical movement can be appreciated for the knowledge, labour and technical decisions involved in its construction.

An elaborate calendar complication can be interesting because of the ingenuity required to make it work.

And a carefully designed mechanical watch can offer a physical connection to a manufacturing tradition that has developed over centuries.

None of these qualities make quartz inferior.

They simply demonstrate that a mechanical watch can be valuable for reasons that have little to do with winning a competition in accuracy.

I think this distinction helped create the conditions in which modern high-end mechanical watchmaking could flourish.

The Problem With Calling Quartz Soulless

There is one argument I hear frequently in watch collecting that I have never found particularly convincing.

The idea that mechanical watches have soul, while quartz watches do not.

I understand where the sentiment comes from.

A mechanical movement has visible components, a balance wheel in motion, and a physical relationship between stored energy and the passage of time.

There is something appealing about that.

But I do not think emotional value belongs exclusively to one type of technology.

Consider the effort involved in creating the original Quartz Astron.

Or the industrial ingenuity behind the first Swatch.

Or the way quartz technology made accurate watches available to people who could never have justified the cost of a fine mechanical timepiece.

These achievements deserve recognition.

A watch can be meaningful because of its design, history, engineering, cultural influence or personal significance.

Its movement is part of that story, but it does not have to determine the entire story.

I would also argue that dismissing quartz overlooks an important part of modern watchmaking history.

The technology changed how watches were manufactured, sold, worn and understood.

It opened new possibilities for design and helped establish the idea that someone might own several watches for different occasions rather than relying on one timepiece for everything.

That is hardly an insignificant contribution.

Perhaps the more useful distinction is not between watches with and without soul.

It is between watches that offer something worth appreciating and those that do not.

And that question applies equally to mechanical and quartz movements.

What the Crisis Actually Changed

Looking back, the quartz crisis did not produce one clear winner.

Japanese manufacturers transformed the global watch industry through innovation and industrial scale.

Swiss manufacturers underwent an extraordinary period of restructuring, consolidation and repositioning.

Quartz became central to everyday timekeeping, while mechanical watches gradually developed a more distinctive place within the luxury market.

The results were not uniform.

Some brands disappeared. Others survived by changing direction. Some embraced quartz, others defended mechanical complexity, and many eventually offered both.

What changed most fundamentally was the relationship between a watch's function and its value.

Before quartz became widespread, the mechanical wristwatch was still closely associated with the practical necessity of keeping time.

After quartz, mechanical watches increasingly had to justify themselves through qualities beyond practical performance.

Design became more important.

Heritage became more commercially significant.

Complications, finishing and movement architecture acquired greater visibility as reasons to appreciate a watch.

And the mechanical watch became, for many people, a deliberate choice rather than the default way of wearing time on the wrist.

Of course, fine mechanical watches were objects of luxury long before 1969.

Quartz did not invent craftsmanship, prestige or collecting.

But it intensified the need to explain why those qualities mattered.

And in doing so, it helped reshape how the modern watch industry communicates its value.

A Watch Is More Than Its Accuracy

I sometimes wonder whether the quartz crisis would be remembered differently if we stopped treating it primarily as a battle between two movements.

The story becomes much more interesting when viewed as a turning point in the meaning of the wristwatch itself.

Quartz demonstrated that precision could become widely accessible.

Swatch showed that affordability and industrial production did not have to eliminate design or personality.

Mechanical watchmakers demonstrated that traditional engineering could remain relevant even when newer technology offered a more efficient solution to the same basic problem.

And collectors eventually found reasons to appreciate all three approaches.

That is what makes the period so important.

The industry was forced to separate the practical function of a watch from the many reasons someone might choose to wear one.

Today, we have access to accurate time almost everywhere.

Our phones, computers and countless other devices can tell us the time without requiring a dedicated object on our wrists.

Yet people continue to buy mechanical watches.

They also continue to buy quartz watches.

Some are drawn to complicated movements. Others appreciate unusual design, historical significance or the comfort of a reliable everyday watch.

And plenty of people simply enjoy owning something beautiful.

I think the quartz crisis helped make that diversity possible.

It was a painful transformation, and one that should never be romanticized at the expense of the people and businesses it affected.

But it also revealed something about watchmaking that remains relevant today.

A watch does not have to be the most accurate way of telling the time to be worth wearing.

And perhaps that is the real legacy of the quartz revolution.

It did not make mechanical watchmaking meaningless.

It made the industry explain what its meaning could be.
