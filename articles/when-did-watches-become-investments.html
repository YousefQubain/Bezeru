When Did Watches Become Investments?

Somewhere along the way, the conversation around luxury watches changed. Collectors began talking less about what they loved and more about what their watches might be worth. But when did a passion for watchmaking become a conversation about financial returns?

There was a time when asking someone about their watch usually led to a conversation about the watch itself.

The movement. The design. The brand's history. Perhaps the story of how they acquired it.

Today, the conversation can take a different direction.

How much did you pay?

What is it worth now?

Do you think the price will go up?

It is difficult to spend time around modern watch collecting without encountering these questions.

And I understand why.

Certain watches have appreciated dramatically. Auction records have made headlines far beyond the watch industry, and references that were once relatively ordinary have become objects of extraordinary financial interest.

But I find something slightly strange about the way we now discuss watches as investments.

The word suggests a degree of predictability that the market rarely offers.

It also changes the relationship between the owner and the object.

A watch bought to be worn is approached differently from one bought to be preserved, stored and eventually sold.

Neither motivation is necessarily wrong.

But I think it is worth asking when the financial value of a watch became such an important part of its identity.

And whether something has been lost in the process.

Collecting Came Before Investing

The idea that watches became collectible only when their prices started rising is misleading.

People have collected timepieces for generations.

Long before online marketplaces and price-tracking platforms existed, enthusiasts were drawn to unusual movements, historical significance, fine craftsmanship and watches associated with particular periods or makers.

Watch auctions also have a longer history than today's market might suggest.

Antiquorum was established in Geneva in 1974 and helped develop the international market for collectible timepieces.

During the early 1980s, the auction house pioneered dedicated wristwatch auctions, giving collectors new opportunities to compare, discover and acquire watches within a more formal marketplace.

Its 1989 thematic auction devoted to Patek Philippe demonstrated how an individual watchmaker's history could become the subject of an entire collecting event.

These developments mattered.

They helped establish that a wristwatch could be appreciated not simply as a useful object or a luxury accessory, but as something with historical and cultural value.

Yet collecting and investing are not quite the same thing.

A collector might spend years searching for a particular reference because of its design, unusual movement or place in watchmaking history.

An investor might acquire the same watch because they believe someone else will eventually pay more for it.

Sometimes these motivations overlap.

A knowledgeable collector can recognize an undervalued watch. An investor can develop a genuine appreciation for what they own.

But the distinction lies in what determines the purchase.

Is the watch desirable because of what it represents?

Or because of what someone expects it to become worth?

I think modern watch collecting has increasingly struggled to separate those two questions.

When Auction Records Became Headlines

On October 26, 2017, a Rolex Daytona belonging to Paul Newman sold at Phillips in New York for $17,752,500.

The result was extraordinary.

The watch was not simply another vintage Daytona. It was Newman's own reference 6239, featuring the exotic dial configuration that collectors had already come to associate with his name.

Its history, ownership and cultural significance made it an exceptional object.

And that is precisely why the auction deserves careful interpretation.

The sale demonstrated how an ordinary manufactured object, through provenance and historical association, could acquire a value far beyond its original purpose.

But it did not establish that every vintage Daytona was suddenly worth millions.

Nor did it demonstrate that buying a modern Rolex was a reliable route to financial appreciation.

Those conclusions require a leap that the auction itself cannot support.

Still, I think events like this helped change the public perception of collectible watches.

A wristwatch selling for nearly $18 million is no longer just a story for people interested in horology.

It becomes a story about wealth, rarity, celebrity and the astonishing prices people are willing to pay for particular objects.

Two years later, another remarkable result reinforced that perception.

At the Only Watch charity auction in November 2019, a unique stainless-steel Patek Philippe Grandmaster Chime, reference 6300A-010, sold for CHF 31 million.

It was a highly complicated, one-of-a-kind watch created specifically for the event.

Again, the circumstances were exceptional.

The piece combined extraordinary technical complexity, uniqueness, brand prestige and the special context of a charity auction.

But headline figures have a way of escaping their original context.

A record price for one historically or technically exceptional watch can easily be mistaken for evidence that watches, as a category, are exceptional financial assets.

And that is where the conversation becomes more complicated.

Scarcity Became Part of the Attraction

Luxury has always involved scarcity.

A complicated movement requires specialized expertise. Certain watches are produced in small quantities. Some historical references survive in relatively few examples.

These are genuine reasons why particular timepieces may be difficult to acquire.

But scarcity can also become a commercial narrative.

A watch that cannot be obtained easily attracts attention. That attention generates demand, and demand can reinforce the perception that the watch must be desirable.

Eventually, the difficulty of purchasing the object becomes part of its appeal.

This is particularly visible when a watch's secondary-market price exceeds its official retail price.

Suddenly, purchasing the watch through an authorized retailer can appear to offer an immediate financial advantage.

But the relationship between price and value is not always straightforward.

A limited-production watch is not automatically significant.

A long waiting list does not guarantee lasting demand.

And a high asking price does not mean someone has actually completed a transaction at that figure.

I think this is where modern watch collecting can become disconnected from the watches themselves.

A person may want a particular reference because it is difficult to obtain, rather than because the design or movement interests them.

The object becomes desirable partly because other people desire it.

That is not unique to watchmaking.

But it is an uncomfortable foundation for something presented as an investment.

Because demand can change much faster than craftsmanship, historical significance or the physical object itself.

The Market Learned to Watch Itself

Another important development was the growing visibility of secondary-market prices.

Online marketplaces, specialist dealers and watch-price tracking services made it increasingly easy to compare references, study price movements and follow collecting trends.

This brought useful transparency to a market that had previously depended heavily on specialist knowledge and personal relationships.

Collectors could research historical sales, compare listings and become better informed before making expensive purchases.

But it also encouraged a different kind of attention.

A watch could now be followed almost like a financial asset.

People began discussing percentage gains, market peaks, price corrections and which references were outperforming others.

The years surrounding the pandemic intensified that relationship.

Demand for certain luxury watches increased sharply before the secondary market experienced a significant correction beginning in 2022.

According to WatchCharts, its overall market index entered a prolonged period of falling prices after that peak.

By 2025, parts of the market were beginning to stabilize, and subsequent data showed a recovery across several major brands.

The picture in 2026 has been more nuanced than a simple boom-or-bust narrative.

In its October 2026 market update, WatchCharts reported that its overall index was virtually unchanged during September. Rolex fell 0.4% for the month, while Audemars Piguet gained 0.5% and Patek Philippe rose 0.2%.

These are movements within a defined market-tracking methodology, not a measure of the price of every watch.

And that qualification matters.

A headline about the luxury watch market can conceal significant differences between brands, collections and individual references.

Some watches become more desirable.

Others lose attention.

Some historically important pieces remain difficult to find regardless of broader trends.

And many watches never command a resale premium in the first place.

To me, the most important lesson from the market's fluctuations is not that watches are poor investments or that appreciation is impossible.

It is that the financial outcome is much less predictable than the stories surrounding successful purchases might suggest.

Even the Brands Recognized the Secondary Market

The growth of the pre-owned market also changed how watchmakers approached watches that had already been sold.

For many years, the secondary market existed largely through dealers, auction houses, independent specialists and private collectors.

Manufacturers focused primarily on producing and selling new watches.

But as interest in pre-owned luxury watches developed, the relationship became harder to ignore.

Rolex offers a useful example.

In 2022, the company introduced its Certified Pre-Owned programme through selected retail partners.

The programme allows eligible previously owned Rolex watches to be authenticated, serviced and sold through participating official retailers with a manufacturer-backed guarantee.

This was a significant development.

It demonstrated that the commercial life of a luxury watch did not necessarily end when it left the original retailer.

A watch could return to an official sales environment years later, carrying value as a pre-owned object.

But I would not interpret this as an endorsement of watches as investments.

Authentication, servicing and resale are not the same as guaranteed financial appreciation.

Instead, the programme reflects something arguably more interesting.

Luxury watchmakers increasingly recognize that their products can remain relevant through multiple owners and across decades.

There is value in that continuity.

And it does not have to be purely financial.

The Costs We Prefer Not to Discuss

One of the biggest problems with describing watches as investments is that people often focus on the difference between the purchase price and a quoted resale value.

That is only part of the calculation.

Buying and selling a watch can involve dealer margins, auction premiums, transaction fees, servicing costs, insurance, shipping and potentially taxes.

The watch's condition matters.

Its originality matters.

The presence of documentation can matter.

A replacement dial, heavily polished case or poorly executed restoration can affect how collectors evaluate an otherwise desirable reference.

Liquidity is another consideration.

A watch may have an impressive estimated market value without there being an immediate buyer prepared to pay it.

Selling quickly may require accepting a lower price.

Selling through a dealer may involve a meaningful difference between the retail asking price and the amount offered to the owner.

And selling at auction introduces its own expenses, uncertainties and timing.

None of this means watches cannot appreciate.

Some clearly have.

But a watch bought for €10,000 and later advertised for €12,000 has not necessarily generated a €2,000 profit for its owner.

The actual result depends on what the owner can receive after the relevant costs.

That distinction is rarely as exciting as an auction record.

It is also much more relevant to someone considering a watch primarily for financial reasons.

Price Is Not the Same as Value

I think one of the most unfortunate consequences of the investment conversation is that it can narrow our understanding of what makes a watch interesting.

A reference that rises dramatically in price begins receiving attention almost automatically.

Another watch, perhaps with an equally thoughtful design or a more unusual movement, can remain overlooked because its resale market is less spectacular.

This creates a strange hierarchy.

Financial performance starts to become a substitute for horological judgment.

And I do not think those things should be confused.

A watch can be expensive without being particularly innovative.

It can be rare without being especially beautiful.

And it can be commercially unsuccessful while representing a fascinating chapter in design or mechanical engineering.

Some of my favourite things about watchmaking have little connection to secondary-market value.

The proportions of a well-designed case.

The architecture of an unusual movement.

The decisions made by an independent watchmaker who refuses to follow a familiar formula.

The way a relatively ordinary watch can capture the identity of a particular decade.

These qualities do not disappear when prices fall.

Similarly, a watch does not become better designed because the market suddenly assigns it a higher value.

Of course, the financial aspect cannot be ignored entirely.

Luxury watches are expensive purchases, and understanding what an object might be worth in the future is a reasonable consideration.

But I would prefer to see resale value treated as one factor in a broader decision, rather than the reason the decision exists.

What Are We Actually Collecting?

Perhaps the most revealing question is a simple one.

Would you still want the watch if its market value fell tomorrow?

Not if it became worthless.

Not if its design changed or its movement stopped working.

Simply if the wider market decided it was less fashionable, less desirable or less financially valuable than it had been the previous month.

Would you still appreciate it?

Would you still enjoy wearing it?

Would it still mean something to you?

I think the answers tell us more about the nature of a collection than any price chart.

There is nothing wrong with being pleased when something you own becomes more valuable.

And there is nothing wrong with considering resale value before making a significant purchase.

Collectors do not have to pretend money is irrelevant.

But if financial appreciation becomes the only measure of success, watch collecting risks losing much of what made it interesting in the first place.

Every watch becomes a position to monitor.

Every purchase becomes a prediction.

And every change in market sentiment becomes a reason to question whether the object still deserves a place in the collection.

That seems like a rather exhausting way to enjoy something designed to be worn on the wrist.

The Watch Should Be Worth Owning Before It Becomes Worth More

So when did watches become investments?

There was no single moment.

The foundations were laid through decades of collecting, specialist auctions and growing recognition of historically important timepieces.

Exceptional auction results brought the market to a wider audience.

Digital platforms made prices more visible.

And a period of extraordinary demand encouraged more people to approach luxury watches through the language of financial returns.

But I would argue that the more important change was cultural.

At some point, we began asking what a watch might be worth before asking why it mattered.

And that reversed the order of a conversation I find much more interesting the other way around.

A watch can preserve remarkable craftsmanship.

It can represent an important historical period, an unusual design decision or a particular watchmaker's philosophy.

It can also carry personal memories that no auction result can measure.

These things are not reflected in a market index.

Nor should they have to be.

I am not against the idea that certain watches can become valuable assets.

History has demonstrated that some do.

But I would be cautious about allowing the promise of future appreciation to become the defining reason for owning them.

Because a financial return is never guaranteed.

A great watch, on the other hand, should offer something worth appreciating long before anyone asks what it might sell for.

And perhaps the healthiest approach to collecting is to remember that distinction.

Buy something because it deserves a place on your wrist.

If it becomes more valuable over time, consider that a welcome development.

But if the price never changes, the watch should still have given you a reason to own it.
